Exercise 13 / Probability rules

Possible overlaps

Given P(A) = 0.6 and P(B) = 0.5, can the overlap be 0.3? Can it be 0.05? What is its smallest possible value?

Hint: The union cannot have probability greater than 1.

Show the worked solution · Exercise 13 · Can these events exist?

Given P(A) = 0.6 and P(B) = 0.5, intersection probabilities must satisfy:

\[\max(0,P(A)+P(B)-1)\le P(A\cap B)\le\min(P(A),P(B)).\]

Thus \(0.1\le P(A\cap B)\le0.5\). (a) 0.3 is possible: take ten equally likely outcomes, A = {1,2,3,4,5,6} and B = {4,5,6,7,8}. (b) 0.05 is impossible: the union would have probability 1.05. (c) The smallest possible intersection is 0.1, achieved by A = {1,2,3,4,5,6} and B = {6,7,8,9,10}.

Exercise 14 / Probability rules

Three-event Venn regions

Given P(A) = 0.4, P(B) = 0.7, P(C) = 0.5, pairwise intersections 0.3, 0.2, 0.4 respectively, and triple intersection 0.1: label all eight Venn regions, find P(A ∩ B ∩ Cᶜ), and check whether the assignments are valid.

Hint: Work outward from the triple intersection.

Show the worked solution · Exercise 14 · Fill a three-event Venn diagram from the center

Begin with A ∩ B ∩ C = 0.1. Subtract that center from each pairwise intersection, then subtract the included regions from each individual event.

Disjoint regionComputed probability
A ∩ B ∩ C0.1
A ∩ B only (outside C)0.3 − 0.1 = 0.2
A ∩ C only (outside B)0.2 − 0.1 = 0.1
B ∩ C only (outside A)0.4 − 0.1 = 0.3
A only0.4 − 0.2 − 0.1 − 0.1 = 0
B only0.7 − 0.2 − 0.3 − 0.1 = 0.1
C only0.5 − 0.1 − 0.3 − 0.1 = 0
Outside all three1 − 0.8 = 0.2

Therefore \(P(A\cap B\cap C^c)=0.2\). These are valid probabilities: all eight disjoint regions are nonnegative and sum to 1. A region may have probability zero.

Exercise 15 / Component sampling

Four components from a box of ten

Three of the ten components are defective. Select four simultaneously and uniformly, without replacement. Find the chance of (a) no defectives, (b) exactly one defective, and (c) at least two defectives.

Hint: all four-component subsets are equally likely. Select defective and working components separately.

Show the worked solution

The sample-space count is \(\binom{10}{4}=210\). There are 3 defective and 7 working components. If X counts defectives:

\[P(X=k)=\frac{\binom3k\binom7{4-k}}{\binom{10}{4}},\qquad k=0,1,2,3.\]

(a) \(\binom74/210=35/210=1/6\approx16.6667\%\).

(b) \(\binom31\binom73/210=105/210=1/2=50\%\).

(c) \(1-P(X=0)-P(X=1)=1-1/6-1/2=1/3\approx33.3333\%\).

Direct check for (c): \([\binom32\binom72+\binom33\binom71]/210=(63+7)/210=1/3\). Four defectives are impossible because the box has only three.

This is a hypergeometric counting pattern; you can solve it with combinations without memorizing the distribution name.

Exercise 16 / Optional extension

A point in a square

Select a point uniformly from a square of side 2. A circle of radius 1 is centered inside it. Find the chance that the point lies inside the circle.

Why this is an extension: the photos use finite sample spaces. Here there are infinitely many points, so equal likelihood is expressed through area.

Show the worked solution

Uniform sampling over the square means probability is proportional to area.

\[P(\text{inside circle})=\frac{\pi(1)^2}{2^2}=\frac{\pi}{4}\approx0.785398.\]

The probability is about 78.54%. Do not divide counts of individual points: both regions contain infinitely many. Including or excluding the circular boundary does not change the probability because it has zero area.

Exercise 17 / PIN codes

Repeated digits: exact vs at least

A four-digit PIN is chosen uniformly from 0000 through 9999. Find the chances of (a) four different digits, (b) exactly two digits being the same, (c) at least two being the same, and (d) no zeros.

Hint: order matters, leading zeros are allowed, and there are \(10^4=10000\) PINs.

Show the worked solution

(a) Four distinct digits: \(10\cdot9\cdot8\cdot7/10000=0.504\).

(b) Interpreting “exactly two digits are the same” as exactly one pair with the other two digits different from each other and from the pair:

\[\frac{10\binom42\cdot9\cdot8}{10000}=0.432.\]

Choose the repeated digit, its two positions, then two distinct other digits for the remaining positions. If your lecturer instead intends to include two-pair PINs such as 1122, add \(\binom{10}{2}\binom42/10000=0.027\), giving 0.459. The one-pair interpretation is used here.

(c) At least one repeated digit is the complement of all distinct: \(1-0.504=0.496\). This includes pairs, triples, and four equal digits.

(d) With zero excluded, each position has nine choices: \(9^4/10^4=0.6561\).

Check (b) against the exactly-one-pair poker calculation: both require preventing additional repeated values.

Exercise 18 / Birthdays

At least two people share a birthday

Use the exercise’s 365-day model: equally likely days, no leap day, and independent birthdays for the selected people. Find the probability for groups of 2, 3, 10, 23, and 50.

Hint: count the complement, where every birthday is different. We seek any matching pair, not a match with one particular person.

Show the worked solution
\[P(\text{at least one match})=1-\frac{365\cdot364\cdots(365-n+1)}{365^n},\quad 1\le n\le365.\]

The first person may have any birthday. To avoid matches, the second has 364 available days, the third 363, and so on. Divide the count of distinct birthday assignments by the count of all assignments.

For more than 365 people, a match is certain under this model. Real birthdays are not perfectly uniform or always independent; these answers belong to the stated simplified model.

Exercise 20 / Reverse the condition

Kings and face cards

Select one card uniformly from 52 cards. What is (a) the probability of a king given a face card (J, Q, K), and (b) the probability of a face card given a king?

Hint: there are 4 kings and 12 face cards; all kings are face cards.

Show the worked solution

Let K = king and F = face card. Because \(K\subseteq F\), \(K\cap F=K\).

\[P(K\mid F)=\frac{4/52}{12/52}=\frac13,\qquad P(F\mid K)=\frac{4/52}{4/52}=1.\]

In (a), one-third of the twelve possible face cards are kings. In (b), every one of the four possible kings is a face card. Reversing the condition changes the denominator.

Exercise 21 / Students, not the factory task

Programming and statistics

Of 100 students, 60 take programming, 40 take statistics, and 25 take both. Choose a student uniformly. Find (a) P(statistics | programming), (b) P(programming | statistics), and (c) P(statistics | not programming).

Hint: fill a two-way table first. Every row and column total should agree with the problem.

Show the table and worked solution
StatisticsNot statisticsTotal
Programming253560
Not programming152540
Total4060100

(a) Among the 60 programming students, 25 take statistics: \(25/60=5/12\approx0.4167\).

(b) Among the 40 statistics students, 25 take programming: \(25/40=5/8=0.625\).

(c) Among the 40 students not taking programming, 15 take statistics: \(15/40=3/8=0.375\).

The denominator in (c) is 40, not 100 or 60. Also, \(P(S\mid P^c)\) is not \(1-P(S\mid P)\): the conditioning group changed.
