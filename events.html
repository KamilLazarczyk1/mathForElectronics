03 / Logic with sets

Combining events

Use the same die example throughout: \(\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\), \(A=\{2,4,6\}\) (even), and \(B=\{4,5,6\}\) (greater than 3).

OperationPlain EnglishExample result
Union \(A\cup B\)A or B or both; at least one happens\(\{2,4,5,6\}\)
Intersection \(A\cap B\)A and B; both happen\(\{4,6\}\)
Complement \(A^c\), also \(\overline A\)Not A; everything in Ω outside A\(\{1,3,5\}\)
Difference \(A\setminus B=A\cap B^c\)A happens but B does not\(\{2\}\)
Exactly one: \((A\cap B^c)\cup(A^c\cap B)\)A or B, excluding their overlap\(\{2,5\}\)

Complement is the mathematical spelling (not “compliment”). It always depends on the sample space: the complement of \([20,50]\) within \([0,100]\) is \([0,20)\cup(50,100]\), not every real number outside that interval.

Mutually exclusive events

A and B are mutually exclusive (disjoint) when they cannot happen in the same trial:

\[A\cap B=\varnothing.\]

For one die roll, “odd” and “even” are disjoint. “Even” and “greater than 3” are not. Disjoint events need not cover Ω: \(\{1\}\) and \(\{2\}\) are disjoint but leave out four outcomes. Complementary events are both disjoint and collectively exhaustive (their union is Ω).

Exclusive does not mean independent. Independence means \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). If A and B are disjoint and both have positive probability, they cannot be independent: observing A rules out B. Independence will be developed in a later lecture.

Probability rules that match the pictures

\[0\le P(A)\le1,\quad P(\Omega)=1,\quad P(\varnothing)=0\] \[P(A^c)=1-P(A)\] \[P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)\]

The last subtraction removes the overlap counted once in P(A) and again in P(B). For disjoint events, the overlap is empty, so simply add their probabilities.

Fair-die example

\(P(A)=3/6\), \(P(B)=3/6\), and \(P(A\cap B)=2/6\). Therefore \(P(A\cup B)=3/6+3/6-2/6=4/6\), matching the four outcomes in the union.

04 / See the logic

Venn diagrams & De Morgan’s rules

A rectangle represents Ω. A circle represents an event. Points inside both circles form the intersection; points outside both are still possible outcomes in Ω. The size of a drawn region does not represent its probability unless explicitly stated.

Explore the shaded event

Same die: A = {2, 4, 6}; B = {4, 5, 6}. Blue shading marks the selected event.

A ∪ B = {2, 4, 5, 6}. At least one event occurs.

De Morgan’s two rules

\[(A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\qquad\text{(neither A nor B)}\] \[(A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\qquad\text{(not both A and B)}\]

How to remember: move the complement through the brackets, complement each event, and swap ∪ with ∩. Negating “at least one” gives “neither.” Negating “both” gives “at least one does not.”

Electronics connection: system failure

Let A = component 1 works and B = component 2 works.

Series system: works when \(A\cap B\). It fails when \((A\cap B)^c=A^c\cup B^c\): at least one component fails.

Parallel system: works when \(A\cup B\). It fails when \((A\cup B)^c=A^c\cap B^c\): both fail.

These are logical descriptions. They do not require component failures to be independent.

Other set rules worth knowing

\[A\cup B=B\cup A,\qquad A\cap B=B\cap A\] \[A\cap(B\cup C)=(A\cap B)\cup(A\cap C)\] \[A\cup(B\cap C)=(A\cup B)\cap(A\cup C)\] \[A\cup A^c=\Omega,\quad A\cap A^c=\varnothing,\quad (A^c)^c=A\]

Union and intersection are also associative: brackets can be regrouped when the operation stays the same. For many events, De Morgan still works: “none of them” is the intersection of all their complements.

Why does the first De Morgan rule work?

Take any outcome ω. If ω is outside A ∪ B, it is not in A and not in B. It therefore belongs to both Aᶜ and Bᶜ. The reasoning reverses too, so the two sets contain exactly the same outcomes.
