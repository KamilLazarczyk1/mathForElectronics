Conditional probability

“Given B” means you now consider only the outcomes in B. Within that smaller group, how often does A also happen?

\[\boxed{P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(A\cap B)}{P(B)}}\qquad P(B)>0.\]

Read the vertical bar as “given”. The denominator is always the event after the bar. The numerator is the overlap: outcomes satisfying both the condition and the target event.

In a finite equally likely sample space, this becomes \(P(A\mid B)=|A\cap B|/|B|\). The equally likely assumption is needed for the counting version, but not for the probability-ratio definition.

Three distinctions: \(P(A\mid B)\) is generally different from P(A), \(P(B\mid A)\), and \(P(A\cap B)\). Conditioning does not say B causes A or happens earlier in time. It says B is known to be true. The elementary ratio is undefined when P(B) = 0.

Board example: choose a number from 1 to 9

Choose uniformly from \(\Omega=\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9\}\). Let A = a multiple of 3 and B = an even number.

\[A=\{3,6,9\},\quad B=\{2,4,6,8\},\quad A\cap B=\{6\}.\]
Before learning anything

Three of the nine outcomes are multiples of 3.

\[P(A)=\frac39=\frac13.\]
Given that the number is even

Only four candidates remain, and just 6 is a multiple of 3.

\[P(A\mid B)=\frac{1/9}{4/9}=\frac14.\]

Reversing the condition gives \(P(B\mid A)=1/3\): among {3, 6, 9}, only one is even. The same overlap has a different denominator.

A always means “multiple of 3.” Select the condition B. Outlined tiles belong to B; filled tiles belong to A ∩ B.

Defects: probability among a known group

Let L = a length defect and T = a texture defect. The board gives 10% with a length defect, 5% with a texture defect, and a handwritten overlap that reads 0.8% with both defects.

The overlap is read as 0.8%, or 0.008 as a probability. Reading it as 8% would be inconsistent: “both defects” cannot be more common than the 5% with a texture defect. The original photo is included so you can compare the handwriting.

Given that a strip has the wrong length, what is the chance it also has the wrong texture?

\[P(T\mid L)=\frac{P(T\cap L)}{P(L)}=\frac{0.008}{0.10}=0.08=8\%.\]

Make the denominator tangible: imagine 10,000 strips with these proportions. Of 1,000 length-defective strips, 80 also have texture defects. The required fraction is 80/1,000, not 80/10,000.

Reverse the condition and the result changes: \(P(L\mid T)=0.008/0.05=0.16=16\%\). Among 500 texture-defective strips, those same 80 have a length defect.

Useful consequences

From “given” back to “both”

Rearranging the definition gives the general multiplication rule:

\[P(A\cap B)=P(B)P(A\mid B)=P(A)P(B\mid A).\]

Use each conditional expression only when its conditioning event has positive probability. You do not need independence for this rule.

Within a fixed condition, the complement rule also holds:

\[P(A^c\mid B)=1-P(A\mid B).\]

It changes the target event, not the condition. In general, \(P(A\mid B^c)\ne1-P(A\mid B)\).

Connection to independence — a preview

If learning B leaves the chance of A unchanged, \(P(A\mid B)=P(A)\), then the multiplication rule becomes \(P(A\cap B)=P(A)P(B)\). The product equality is the general definition of independence, including cases with zero probabilities. The photos do not show a full treatment of independence or Bayes’ theorem, so their dedicated exercises are reserved for later.

A reliable four-step method

  1. Name the target event and the given event.
  2. Put the given event after the bar and in the denominator.
  3. Find the intersection from the data, a set, or a table.
  4. Divide and check that the result is between 0 and 1.
