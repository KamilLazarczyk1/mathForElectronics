Lecture 2 / Counting equally likely hands

Poker: exactly one pair or at least one?

These sound similar, but count different sets of hands. The key is to describe the hand precisely before multiplying choices.

A standard deck has 13 ranks (2–10, J, Q, K, A) and 4 suits. A five-card hand is an unordered set of five distinct cards, dealt without replacement. Assume all such hands are equally likely:

\[N=\binom{52}{5}=2{,}598{,}960,\qquad P(\text{hand type})=\frac{\text{number of hands of that type}}{N}.\]

Check your understanding

Count hands rather than dealing orders

0/3 solved

  1. Why does a five-card hand use C(52, 5), rather than P(52, 5)?

    Answer: The same five cards form the same hand in any order A hand is an unordered subset of distinct cards. P(52, 5) would count each hand 5! times.

  2. A miniature deck has 8 distinct cards. How many unordered three-card hands are possible?

    Answer: 56 8 × 7 × 6 / (3 × 2 × 1) = 56.

  3. In a standard deck, how many rank choices and suit choices does one card have?

    Answer: 13 ranks and 4 suits Thirteen ranks times four suits gives 52 distinct cards.

At least one repeated rank

Count the complement: five different ranks. Choose those ranks and then one of four suits for each.

\[N(\text{all ranks different})=\binom{13}{5}4^5=1{,}317{,}888.\]\[N(\text{at least one pair of equal ranks})=\binom{52}{5}-\binom{13}{5}4^5=1{,}281{,}072.\]

The probability is about 49.2917%. Here “at least one pair” means that some two cards share a rank, so it also includes two pairs, three of a kind, full houses, and four of a kind. It is not the poker category called “one pair.”

Why the tempting board expression overcounts: \(13\binom42\binom{50}{3}\) chooses a marked pair and then any three remaining cards. A hand with two pairs can be generated by marking either pair; three of a kind contains three possible marked pairs. These unequal multiplicities mean this is not a count of distinct hands. Use the complement above.

Check your understanding

At least one repeated rank

0/3 solved

  1. What is the complement of “some two cards share a rank”?

    Answer: All five ranks are distinct If no two cards share a rank, all five ranks must be distinct.

  2. A miniature deck has 5 ranks and 2 suits per rank. How many three-card hands have all ranks distinct?

    Answer: 80 C(5, 3) × 2³ = 10 × 8 = 80.

  3. For the same 10-card deck, how many three-card hands have at least one repeated rank?

    Answer: 40 C(10, 3) − 80 = 120 − 80 = 40. There are only two suits, so a triple of one rank is impossible.

Exactly one pair

  1. Choose the pair’s rank: 13 ways.
  2. Choose two suits for that rank: \(\binom42=6\) ways.
  3. Choose three different other ranks from the remaining twelve: \(\binom{12}{3}\) ways.
  4. Choose a suit for each of those three ranks: \(4^3\) ways.
\[N(\text{one pair})=13\binom42\binom{12}{3}4^3=1{,}098{,}240.\]\[P(\text{one pair})=\frac{1{,}098{,}240}{2{,}598{,}960}\approx42.2569\%.\]

Requiring three distinct other ranks prevents a second pair, a triple, or a fourth card of the pair’s rank. We do not multiply by 5!: changing the order of these cards does not create a new hand.

Prevent extra repeated ranks

0/3 solved

  1. In a standard-deck hand with exactly one pair, how should the other three ranks be chosen?

    Answer: Three different ranks from the twelve remaining ranks Choose three distinct ranks from the twelve ranks different from the pair’s rank.

  2. From a miniature deck with 5 ranks and 2 suits, how many four-card hands have exactly one pair?

    Answer: 120 5 × C(4, 2) × 2² = 5 × 6 × 4 = 120.

  3. If instead you count four-card hands with two pairs in that miniature deck, how many are there?

    Answer: 10 C(5, 2) = 10. The two pair ranks are an unordered selection.

Understand the factors

Two pairs, three of a kind, and full house

Two pairs: choose two ranks without ordering them; choose two suits for each; choose the fifth card’s rank from the eleven unused ranks and then its suit. Using 13 × 12 for the pair ranks would count each hand twice.

Three of a kind: choose the triple rank and its three suits, then two distinct other ranks and their suits. The two remaining ranks must differ to exclude a full house.

Full house: the triple and pair play different roles. Choose the triple rank and suits, then one of twelve other ranks and two of its suits. There is no division by 2 for these distinct roles.

Straights, flushes, high cards, and four of a kind

Straight: for each of ten rank sequences, choose one suit per rank. Exclude the four choices where every suit is the same.

Flush: choose a suit and any five ranks, then exclude the ten straight rank patterns in that suit. This removes all straight flushes, including royals.

High card: choose five distinct ranks that do not form a straight, then a suit pattern that is not a flush. Pairs are already excluded by distinct ranks.

Four of a kind: choose the rank; all four suits are forced. The fifth card can be any of the 48 cards of other ranks.

Straight flush vs royal: there are 40 straight flushes if royals are included. The task lists royal flush separately, so split them into 36 and 4.

Make the categories disjoint

0/3 solved

  1. A hand has three queens and two sevens. What is its category?

    Answer: Full house A full house has one triple and one pair. The “three of a kind” category requires the other two ranks to differ.

  2. The ten standard straight rank patterns occur in four suits. If royal flushes are a separate category, how many straight flushes remain?

    Answer: 36 10 × 4 − 4 = 36 non-royal straight flushes.

  3. Which statements are true for the task list’s categories?

    Answer: A full house is excluded from exactly one pair.; The ten category counts sum to all five-card hands.; “At least one repeated rank” includes four of a kind. The table partitions all hands into disjoint categories. Royals are counted separately; full houses are not exactly one pair; four of a kind does contain a repeated rank.
