05 / Count once, count correctly

Combinatorics & permutations

Combinatorics counts possible arrangements and selections. First decide what makes two outcomes different.

Two basic counting principles

Multiplication rule: if a process has stages with \(n_1,n_2,\ldots,n_k\) choices at each stage (the stated number available for every preceding choice), the total is \(n_1n_2\cdots n_k\). Three letters from a seven-letter alphabet followed by four digits give \(7^3\cdot10^4\) IDs when repetition is allowed.

Addition rule: if a result belongs to one of several disjoint cases, add the counts for the cases. If cases overlap, separate them or correct the double-counting.

The multiplication rule for counting does not require probabilistic independence. Without replacement, choices change with each stage, but you can still multiply 8 × 7 × 6 × 5 × 4.

Check your understanding

Add alternatives, multiply stages

0/3 solved

  1. A device comes in 3 colors and 4 sizes. Every color has every size. How many color–size choices are there?

    Answer: 12 3 × 4 = 12 combinations of these two design choices.

  2. Choose exactly one connector: either one of 5 distinct USB connectors or one of 3 distinct coaxial connectors. How many choices?

    Answer: 8 5 + 3 = 8. Multiplication would describe choosing one of each type, which is a different task.

  3. Does the multiplication rule for counting require probabilistic independence?

    Answer: No The counting rule multiplies the number of available choices at successive stages. It does not assume anything about probabilities.

Factorials

\[n!=n(n-1)\cdots2\cdot1,\qquad 0!=1.\]

For a nonnegative integer n, n! counts ways to arrange n distinct objects in a line. For example, three different components A, B, C have \(3!=6\) orders: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA. There is one way to arrange no objects, which explains the convention 0! = 1.

Arrange all the objects

0/3 solved

  1. Five distinct circuit modules are arranged in a line. How many orders are possible?

    Answer: 120 5! = 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120.

  2. Simplify 7! / 5!.

    Answer: 42 7!/5! = (7 × 6 × 5!)/5! = 42.

  3. Why does 0! equal 1 in counting?

    Answer: There is one empty arrangement There is one empty arrangement. The convention 0! = 1 keeps the counting formulas consistent.

Choose a counting model

0/3 solved

  1. Choose 4 students from 9 for a team with no assigned roles. Which count fits?

    Answer: C(9, 4) Order does not matter and students cannot be repeated, so use C(9, 4).

  2. Make a three-symbol code from five symbols, with repeats allowed. Which count fits?

    Answer: 5³ There are 5³ = 125 ordered codes. Repetition is allowed, so the choices do not decrease.

  3. From 9 people choose a chair, a secretary, and a treasurer, all different people. Which count fits?

    Answer: P(9, 3) Role assignments are ordered selections without repetition: 9 × 8 × 7 = P(9, 3).

Ordered selections without repetition

\[P(n,k)=n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)=\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}.\]

These are often called k-permutations, arrangements, or variations without repetition. Some courses reserve “permutations” for arranging all n objects; then the count is n!. The counting logic is the same.

Drone flight plans — List 1, checkpoint Exercise 6

A drone visits five different checkpoints from eight. ABCDE and BACDE are different routes, so order matters. Once a checkpoint is used, it cannot be chosen again:

\[P(8,5)=8\cdot7\cdot6\cdot5\cdot4=6720.\]

Using \(8^5\) would allow repeat visits. Using \(\binom85\) would count sets of checkpoints and ignore the route order.

Fill positions without repetition

0/3 solved

  1. Visit 3 different stations from 7, recording their visit order. How many routes?

    Answer: 210 7 × 6 × 5 = 210 routes.

  2. Assign 4 distinct modules to 4 of 6 labeled slots, at most one per slot. How many assignments?

    Answer: 360 6 × 5 × 4 × 3 = 360. The module identities distinguish the assignments.

  3. Why is C(7, 3) too small for ordered three-station routes?

    Answer: It ignores the 3! orders of each chosen group Each three-station subset has 3! = 6 orders. P(7, 3) = C(7, 3) × 3!.

Permutations when some objects are identical

If n objects include groups of identical objects of sizes \(n_1,\ldots,n_r\), with their sum equal to n, the number of distinct arrangements is:

\[\frac{n!}{n_1!n_2!\cdots n_r!}.\]

For AABC there are \(4!/2!=12\) distinct strings: exchanging the two A’s does not create a new string. This is different from choosing freely with repetition, which would allow the number of A’s to change.

Avoid counting swaps of identical objects

0/3 solved

  1. How many distinct strings can be made by rearranging AAB?

    Answer: 3 3!/2! = 3: AAB, ABA, BAA.

  2. How many distinct strings can be made by rearranging AABBCC?

    Answer: 90 6!/(2! × 2! × 2!) = 720/8 = 90.

  3. Which problem uses 6!/(3!2!1!)?

    Answer: Arrange a fixed collection of 3 red, 2 blue, and 1 green identical-within-color tokens The formula arranges six tokens with fixed color counts. Free repetition permits the counts to vary and needs a different model.

06 / Unordered selection

Newton’s symbol & the binomial theorem

Newton’s symbol (Polish: symbol Newtona) is the binomial coefficient \(\binom nk\), read “n choose k.” It counts k-element subsets of an n-element set of distinct objects.

\[\boxed{\binom nk=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}}\qquad 0\le k\le n.\]

Why divide by k!? The ordered-selection count \(P(n,k)\) counts every chosen group k! times, once for each possible order. Divide out those rearrangements to count the group once.

Choose 2 components from A, B, C, D

There are 4 × 3 = 12 ordered selections. But AB and BA describe the same selected pair. Divide by 2! to get 6 pairs: AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, CD.

For List 1’s 12 distinct components, selecting 4 gives \(\binom{12}{4}=495\). Selecting exactly 2 of the 5 resistors and 2 of the 4 capacitors gives \(\binom52\binom42=10\cdot6=60\).

Check your understanding

Count an unordered selection

0/3 solved

  1. Select 2 distinct test points from 7, with no order. How many selections?

    Answer: 21 C(7, 2) = 7 × 6 / 2 = 21.

  2. Choose 3 distinct components from 8 for a test batch. How many batches?

    Answer: 56 C(8, 3) = (8 × 7 × 6)/(3 × 2 × 1) = 56.

  3. Why is P(n, k) divided by k! to obtain C(n, k)?

    Answer: Each chosen set appears in k! orders All k! rearrangements represent the same unordered selection, so division removes the repeated counting.

Useful identities

\[\binom n0=\binom nn=1,\qquad \binom n1=n,\qquad \binom nk=\binom n{n-k}\] \[\binom nk=\binom{n-1}{k-1}+\binom{n-1}{k}\quad(1\le k\le n-1).\]

The symmetry identity says choosing the included objects is equivalent to choosing the excluded ones. Pascal’s identity splits groups into those that contain a particular object and those that do not. This generates Pascal’s triangle:

Use the identities

0/3 solved

  1. What is C(9, 0)?

    Answer: 1 The empty subset is a valid, unique selection, so C(9, 0) = 1.

  2. Use symmetry to find C(10, 8).

    Answer: 45 C(10, 8) = C(10, 2) = 45.

  3. Pascal’s identity gives C(7, 3) = C(6, 2) + C(6, 3). What is the result?

    Answer: 35 15 + 20 = 35. The cases split selections according to whether a specified object is included.

Why “binomial”?

\[(a+b)^n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom nk a^{n-k}b^k.\]

n is a nonnegative integer and k runs from 0 to n. Expanding n factors of (a + b), a term with k copies of b is formed by choosing which k factors contribute b. That is why its coefficient is \(\binom nk\).

\[(a+b)^4=a^4+4a^3b+6a^2b^2+4ab^3+b^4.\]

Setting a = b = 1 gives \(\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk=2^n\): an n-element set has 2ⁿ subsets because each element is either included or excluded.

Coefficients count choices

0/3 solved

  1. What is the coefficient of a³b² in (a + b)⁵?

    Answer: 10 C(5, 2) = 10. The other three factors contribute a.

  2. How many subsets does a six-element set have, including the empty set?

    Answer: 64 2⁶ = 64 subsets. Equivalently, sum C(6, k) from k = 0 through 6.

  3. Which term in (a + b)⁵ corresponds to choosing b from four factors?

    Answer: 5ab⁴ The term is C(5, 4)ab⁴ = 5ab⁴.
