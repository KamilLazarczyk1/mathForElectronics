01 / The big picture

Models & statistical behavior

A model is a simplified description of a system. We choose a model according to what we know and what we want to predict.

Deterministic model

Once its inputs, parameters, and initial conditions are fixed, the model gives a single outcome. There is no random element inside the model.

Example: an ideal 1 kΩ resistor with exactly 5 V across it has current \(I=V/R=5\text{ mA}\).

Random (stochastic) model

The model allows different outcomes and describes their probabilities. It predicts how outcomes are distributed, rather than guaranteeing one result.

Example: manufactured “1 kΩ” resistors vary. A randomly selected resistor has an uncertain resistance; a model can describe that variation.

These are modeling choices, not permanent labels for a device. A circuit may be modeled deterministically for its nominal response and randomly for noise, component tolerances, or transmission errors.

Check your understanding

Deterministic or random?

0/3 solved

  1. A model uses exactly 12 V and exactly 4 Ω to calculate a current. What kind of model is it?

    Answer: Deterministic The model gives I = 12/4 = 3 A every time. Measurement variation could be added in a separate random model.

  2. A sensor model adds a randomly varying noise value to its ideal output. What does it predict?

    Answer: A range of possible readings with probabilities The reading is uncertain, but the model can describe its distribution and the likelihood of different readings.

  3. Which statements about models are valid? Select all that apply.

    Answer: A deterministic model can approximate a real noisy system.; The same device can have deterministic and random models.; A random model can describe long-run patterns. Models are chosen for a purpose. Ideal circuit behavior can be deterministic while noise is random; random outcomes need not be equally likely.

What is statistical behavior?

Individual results can be unpredictable while the pattern across many trials is comparatively stable. We study frequencies, averages, and variation across repeated observations.

\[f_N(A)=\frac{N_A}{N}\]

Here \(N\) is the number of trials, \(N_A\) counts how often event \(A\) occurred, and \(f_N(A)\) is its relative frequency. This observed frequency estimates the model probability \(P(A)\).

For independent repetitions under the same conditions, the law of large numbers says the relative frequency converges to the event’s probability in a precise probabilistic sense. It does not promise an exact proportion in every batch, or that the estimate improves after every trial.

Common trap: a fair coin that has landed heads five times is not “due” for tails. If tosses are independent, the next toss still has probability 1/2 of heads. Long-run balance does not correct past results.

Try it: short runs vs long runs

Simulated independent fair-coin tosses. This is an illustration, not experimental evidence about a physical coin.

Check your understanding

Observed frequency vs probability

0/3 solved

  1. A test detects 18 faulty components in 120 trials. What is the observed relative frequency of a fault?

    Answer: 0.15 (15%) The relative frequency is 18/120 = 0.15. This estimates a probability; it does not prove the true fault rate is exactly 0.15.

  2. An independent fair coin has just given four tails. What is the probability of heads next?

    Answer: 1/2 The next probability remains 1/2. A short streak does not change the fair-coin model.

  3. What can a long sequence of independent, identically distributed trials tell you? Select all valid statements.

    Answer: Relative frequencies tend to stabilize near their probabilities.; Finite samples can still differ from the model probability. The law of large numbers describes convergence. It does not guarantee exact batch proportions or steady improvement after every observation.

02 / The vocabulary

Random experiments, sample spaces & events

A random experiment is a procedure with an uncertain outcome: test three components, transmit a message, or measure a sensor. Specify what you record before listing its outcomes.

TermMeaningOne die roll
Outcome \(\omega\)One possible complete result\(\omega=4\)
Sample space \(\Omega\), also \(S\)The set of all possible outcomes\(\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}\)
Event \(A\)A collection of outcomes; it occurs if the actual outcome belongs to it“Even”: \(A=\{2,4,6\}\)
Elementary eventAn event containing one outcome\(\{4\}\), rather than the outcome \(4\) itself
Impossible / certain eventEmpty set / entire sample space\(\varnothing\) / \(\Omega\)

Write \(\omega\in A\) for “outcome ω belongs to A” and \(A\subseteq B\) for “every outcome in A is also in B.” If \(A\subseteq B\), then whenever A happens, B happens too. The notation \(|A|\) means the number of elements of a finite set A.

Three component tests

Record the results in order, using W = works and F = fails:

\[\Omega=\{WWW,WWF,WFW,WFF,FWW,FWF,FFW,FFF\}.\]

“Exactly two work” is \(\{WWF,WFW,FWW\}\). WWF and FWW are different outcomes because different components failed. There are \(2^3=8\) outcomes, but counting alone does not make them equally likely.

Check your understanding

Outcome, sample space, event

0/3 solved

  1. For one die roll, which expression is the event “less than 3”?

    Answer: {1, 2} Both 1 and 2 satisfy “less than 3”, so the event is {1, 2}.

  2. Two lamps are tested in order. Select every outcome where exactly one lamp works (W = works, F = fails).

    Answer: WF; FW WF and FW each contain one working lamp. They are different outcomes because the lamp positions are different.

  3. How many outcomes are in the impossible event?

    Answer: 0 The empty set contains no outcomes. Its size and its probability are both zero, though size and probability are different concepts.

The same system can have different sample spaces

If you record only the number of working components, the space becomes \(\{0,1,2,3\}\). You have discarded position information. Even if the eight ordered W/F strings were equally likely, these four counts would not be: three strings give the count 2, while only one gives the count 3.

Check your understanding

What are you recording?

0/3 solved

  1. Four switches can each be ON or OFF. If you record the ordered switch states, how many outcomes are possible?

    Answer: 16 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16 ordered state strings.

  2. For those four switches, how many different outcomes are possible if you record only the number of ON switches?

    Answer: 5 The count can be 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4, so the reduced sample space has five outcomes.

  3. Suppose all sixteen switch-state strings are equally likely. Are the five ON-counts equally likely?

    Answer: No: different counts correspond to different numbers of strings There is one all-OFF string, but four strings with exactly one ON switch. Collapsing outcomes does not preserve equal probabilities.

Discrete vs continuous

  • Finite: a sensor reporting whole degrees from 0 to 100 has \(\Omega=\{0,1,\ldots,100\}\), with 101 possibilities.
  • Countably infinite: the trial number of the first success can be \(\{1,2,3,\ldots\}\), assuming success eventually occurs with probability 1.
  • Continuous: an ideal sensor reporting any real temperature from 0 to 100 has \(\Omega=[0,100]\), with uncountably many possibilities.

In introductory finite models, every subset is an event. In continuous probability, events formally belong to a specified collection of measurable subsets; ordinary intervals and their unions are valid events.

Counting is not always probability. The formula \(P(A)=|A|/|\Omega|\) applies to a finite, equally likely sample space. It does not apply to arbitrary component outcomes or by counting individual points in a continuous interval.

Check your understanding

Discrete, continuous, and equally likely

0/3 solved

  1. A meter reports 0, 0.5, 1.0, …, 5.0 V. How many distinct readings can it report?

    Answer: 11 There are 5/0.5 = 10 increments and 11 readings including both 0 and 5.

  2. An ideal meter can report any real voltage between 0 and 5. Its sample space is…

    Answer: Continuous The space [0, 5] is continuous and uncountable, unlike a meter restricted to fixed decimal steps.

  3. When is favorable outcomes / total outcomes a valid probability calculation? Select all valid cases.

    Answer: Selecting one component uniformly from 20 distinct components; Choosing one binary string uniformly from all length-3 strings Uniform selection from a finite set supports the count ratio. Unequal face probabilities need weights; continuous geometric regions need areas rather than point counts.
